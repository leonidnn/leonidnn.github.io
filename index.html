Pet or Threat, behaviour of a Unitree Go1 robot dog

October 2026
Personal project, MuJoCoMoscow, Russia
▶ Watch the videoleonidnn.github.io/pet-or-threat-site
  • A simulated robot dog that nobody gives commands to. It decides on its own what to do, for example guard, play, cuddle, wait for its owner or sleep.
  • The key architectural idea is that the decision module receives only perception and emits only intents such as go there or look here, while walking is handled by pretrained RL policies. So the module does not depend on the simulator and moves to another robot by replacing the lower level.

What the dog does

Four needs (energy, social, curiosity, territory), three emotions and what the dog sees and hears decide its mode ten times a second. Pick a mode to watch a clip.

Architecture

The system is split into three levels, like a living animal: senses, brain and muscles. Each level runs at its own rate and talks to its neighbour only through a short message. Below, in order, is what each level does and why it is built that way.

1. Perception500 times a second

What the dog sees, hears and feels

MuJoCo computes the physics, and people in the room walk according to a pre-written script. The dog does not get a ready-made picture of the world. It sees only what falls into its 240° field of view, with noise, and does not see anything hidden behind a wall or another person. It hears bangs and footsteps within 4 m. It feels touch in five body zones and tells a stroke from a pat or a shove.

Touch took some work. People’s hands in simulation are infinitely stiff, and the very first stroke pressed the dog into the floor with 5 kN. I had to make a hand that yields and settles at about 8 N, like a real palm.

The brain receives a perception snapshot: where everyone is as far as the dog knows, what it heard, where it was touched.
2. Decisions10 times a second

What to do right now

The dog has four needs: energy, social contact, curiosity and a sense of territory. They change over time and with events: play makes the dog tired, it misses its owner when alone, a new object makes it curious. On top of the needs there are three emotions: arousal, contentment and fear.

For each mode the brain computes a single number, how appropriate that mode is right now. The mode with the highest number wins. To keep the dog from flipping between two modes, the current mode gets a small head start and a minimum time it must stay in. Reflexes, meaning a startle, a shove or a fall, interrupt anything. Inside the chosen mode a step-by-step routine runs: for sleep, walk to the bed, turn round, lie down and curl up.

Why not a state machine or a neural network. A state machine with seven modes turns into a web of transitions, and it is hard to express “tired, but someone is at the door”. Reinforcement learning would need a reward for dog-likeness, and there is no such thing. Scoring appropriateness solves both problems and also explains every decision. Here is a real line from the log:

hold the line: sentry 1.07 (threat 1.00, territorial 0.98) > idle 0.12

An example of a subtlety. The robot walks half as fast as a person, so the dog starts guarding as soon as a stranger could reach the target within 8 seconds, rather than when the stranger comes within some distance. Otherwise it simply does not make it in time.

The body receives an intent: where to go or which pose to take, where to look, whether to bark or growl.
3. Body50 times a second

How to carry it out

Walking and getting up after a fall are driven by pretrained neural RL policies for the Go1 from the open project mjx-go1-getup. Sit, lie down, bow and shake off are predefined poses. Tail, neck and breathing run constantly in the background and show emotions: joy lifts the tail, fear tucks it in.

I did not write my own gait, because the project is about behaviour, not walking. I tested the pretrained policies separately: no falls over 15 commands of 30 seconds each, and a successful get-up in 90% of 40 falls. Porting uncovered details that made the robot fall within the first second, for example an extra input normalization that was not used in training. I never blend poses and walking: the policy authors measured that blending raised the fall rate from 8% to 38–74%. A shove is also caught by the walk policy: it steps to recover, while a frozen pose simply falls over.

The key point of this design. The brain knows nothing about the simulator. It receives a perception snapshot and gives back an intent. So the same brain runs in a simplified sandbox for tests, and moving it to another robot only requires replacing the first and third levels.

Limitations

  • People are kinematic and touch the dog only with their hands. Whether an intruder gets through is decided by an explicit deterrence model, so guarding metrics measure the dog’s positioning and timing.
  • The Go1 walk policy cannot walk backwards or sideways and tops out at 0.5 m/s. Stepping back is a pose.
  • Perception takes ground-truth positions from the simulator through a sensor model with field of view, occlusion, noise and dropouts, not a learned detector.

MIPT CCM, RL agent for RGB-D object grasping

September 2026 – October 2026
Research project, Isaac Sim, UR10eMoscow, Russia
▶ Watch the video and codegithub.com/leonidnn/isaac-rgbd-grasping
  • Built an Isaac Sim environment for grasp training with object randomization. Wrote a custom Vulkan layer that keeps the renderer off other people’s GPUs on the shared server.
  • Trained an RL agent. A network predicts grasp success for every point and angle from a depth map, and the agent takes the best one.
  • Evaluation in new random poses with the full arm approach gave 18/20 and 19/20 on two objects. SAC did not learn at all in 5.7k attempts.
  • Added a new object, a bag of chips, and compared fine-tuning approaches that avoid forgetting the old ones. The best so far is two networks with a switch by object type, giving 88% on the new object versus 42% without fine-tuning while keeping 95% on the old ones.
  • Found a rendering artefact that had distorted training, and fixed the setup.
More

Task. One of three objects lies on the table, a juice carton, a can or a bag of chips. A camera takes a top-down image, the agent decides where and at what angle to grasp, and the arm grasps from above and lifts. Success means lifting by 10 cm and holding for 1 s.

Every episode is randomized. The object, its position on the table, rotation from 0 to 360° and pose all change, the carton stands or lies, the chips face up or down.

Everything happens in simulation. Full arm approach means the arm travels from its home pose using my planner instead of being teleported to the object, as in the fast training environment.

How the agent works

A Q-map following Zeng et al. 2018, Visual Pushing-Grasping, with a much simpler network. An episode is a single step, so training reduces to Q-learning with a horizon of one. After each attempt we know whether the grasp succeeded, and the network output at that cell is pulled towards 1 or 0.

Training

BCE on the chosen cell, replay buffer, ε-greedy from 0.5 to 0.1 over 8 hours, Adam 1e-4.

Fast environment

8 tables in one process, the arm teleports 10 cm above the target point. One attempt takes 1–2 s instead of 1–2 minutes.

SAC

The actor outputs x, y and angle directly. Within 5.7k attempts it collapsed to “always the table centre”.

The work step by step

Each step started by checking that the previous one worked. Every run is kept in the repository with the code of its version, failed ones included.

The rendering artefact

After the first night of training neither the Q-map nor SAC had learned. In the visualizations the colour frame and the depth showed different scenes. A table with a can showed a box in colour, and the colours were washed out.

The hypothesis was that the RTX renderer blends a new frame with previous ones, and I rendered only twice after moving the objects. The test showed that after 2 renders the frame is entirely from the previous scene, after 4 it is a mix, and from 8 on it is correct. Depth is correct immediately.

The fix is 10 renders before each capture and depth only as the network input.

In the second attempt I changed several things at once, so I do not attribute all the improvement to the bug. Still, the same network right after switching the input, before any fine-tuning, succeeded 9 times out of 19, so it had learned something from depth overnight.

A new object without forgetting the old ones

Nobody could grasp the original chips bag, not even the oracle, because it is wider than the gripper opening. I shrank the bag to 6×2×8.7 cm. The oracle grasps it 18 times out of 18, the old agent without fine-tuning 42% of the time.

I compared six fine-tuning approaches under the same budget of 6,400 attempts, about 5 hours on a 2080 Ti, followed by 64 attempts per object. Hypotheses were written down before the results came in.

FiLM with an object code and replay with 20% old objects in training keep all three objects at 88–94%, on par with two separate networks. Without old objects in training, as in naive fine-tuning and adapters, forgetting is severe and fast. One seed and 64 attempts per object, the intervals of the top three variants overlap.

Hypotheses and outcomes

Naive fine-tuning will learn chips fastest but forget the old objects partly
Best on chips, 95%, and forgetting was instant. By 800 attempts the carton was at 12 of 32 and the can at 7 of 32. But the prediction that the standing carton would barely suffer was wrong. In the end the carton dropped more than the can, 39% versus 56%.
With replay, forgetting will be an early dip rather than a slow drift for the can
The can dipped by 800 attempts and recovered to 29–30 of 32. The carton dropped and stayed down, which looks more like drift. Replay was the fastest to reach 80% on chips, by 800 attempts.
FiLM ends up like replay but with a deeper early dip first part right
Final 94, 94 and 89%, close to replay. The early dip on the can was the same as replay’s.
Adapters will barely forget wrong
The can fell to 2 of 32 by 1,600 attempts and ended at 55%. Freezing the backbone is not enough, the adapters act on every object, and the correction for chips breaks the old ones.
Training from scratch on all objects will be worst but balanced half right
Worst on chips, 78%. Not balanced though, the can is at 59%, and training was unstable, everything collapsed at 1,600 attempts and then recovered.

The Vulkan layer and the shared server

GPUs with RT cores, needed to render the camera, exist on only one lab server, two RTX 2080 Ti. The other GPUs are A100s used by other people.

  • glibc. The server runs Ubuntu 20.04, while Isaac Sim 4.5 needs glibc 2.34. I patched the Python binary with patchelf and supplied libraries via LD_LIBRARY_PATH to my process only.
  • Vulkan. The renderer sees all 8 GPUs and ignores CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES, on the first run it took someone else’s A100. I wrote a Vulkan layer that hides other GPUs from the process.
  • Neighbours. Jobs run only through run.sh, with a memory check, GPU lock, tmux and a watchdog that kills the job if it threatens others’ memory. A queue, wait_run.sh, waits for a free GPU and resumes training from a backup after a crash.

NB-Technologies, Hybrid Solver

September 2025 – November 2025
LLM developerMoscow, Russia
▶ Try the solverhuggingface.co/spaces/leonid-nn/hybrid-solver
▶ See the codegithub.com/leonidnn/hybrid-solver
  • A math problem solver that either gives the correct answer or honestly says it could not get one.
  • The key idea is to turn a language model from a Monte Carlo algorithm, which always answers but is sometimes wrong, into a Las Vegas algorithm, as in the ZPP class. Three independent methods solve the problem, model reasoning, exact computation in SymPy and a numerical check, and an answer is given only if all of them agree.
More

The idea

A language model solving a problem behaves like a Monte Carlo probabilistic algorithm. It always gives an answer, but sometimes a wrong one, and a wrong answer looks just as confident as a right one. For a learning tool this is the worst case: a student cannot check the answer, and an error is presented as truth.

Complexity theory has the class ZPP, problems solved by a Las Vegas algorithm. Such an algorithm never gives a wrong answer and instead sometimes says “don’t know”. The project carries this idea over to a language model. The model can still make mistakes, but the solver is built so that a mistake does not reach the user.

How it works

Problem analysistopic, answer type, whether a drawing is needed
→
Reasoningstep-by-step solution, like a teacher
+
Exact computationSymPy code, exact arithmetic
+
Numerical checkbrute force, Monte Carlo, numerical methods
→
Comparisonall agree, an answer; otherwise, a refusal

The three methods run in parallel and know nothing about each other’s answers. Their errors come from different sources: reasoning fails on logic and arithmetic, symbolic code fails on the setup but computes exactly, and the numerical check cannot do clever reasoning but brute force is immune to logical mistakes. For a user to see a wrong answer, all three methods must fail, and fail in the same way.

Answers are compared in SymPy: √109, sqrt(109) and 10.4403065 count as one answer, and the order of roots does not matter. If code crashes, the model fixes it once from the error message, still without seeing the other methods’ answers.

When an answer is given

SituationWhat the user sees
At least two methods answered, all answers agree, at least one is exactThe answer and a drawing
Methods gave different answers“Could not get a reliable answer”
Only one method answered“Could not get a reliable answer”, nothing to confirm it with
A proof problemThe solution is marked as unverified

The solver never picks the “more likely” of disagreeing answers.

Drawings from the computation

In geometry problems the exact-solution code not only computes the answer but also outputs the figure in coordinates, and the server renders it as SVG. The drawing is the same configuration the code computed the answer on, so if the code misread the problem, it shows in the picture. For calculus problems it plots functions and shaded regions.

Experiments and limitations

How close the solver gets to ZPP is measured on three problem sets with reference answers: school problems from Russian MATH-500, university problems from U-MATH and olympiad problems from OlympiadBench. The main metrics are the accuracy of given answers and coverage, how often the solver answers at all. For comparison, the same model is scored without the cross-check, like an ordinary chatbot.

There is no strict zero-error guarantee: one model produces all methods, and if it misreads the problem the same way, the methods can agree on a wrong answer. Next steps are different models for different methods and a rerun on disagreement.
